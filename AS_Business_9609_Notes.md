# TOPIC 1 — BUSINESS AND ITS ENVIRONMENT

## 1.1 Enterprise

### 1.1.1 The nature of business activity

**Purpose of business activity**
- A business combines resources to produce goods/services that satisfy customers' **needs and wants**.
- Core purpose: to identify needs, obtain resources, produce output, and sell it — usually to make a **profit** (private sector) or provide a **service** (public sector).

**Factors of production (LLCE)** — resources needed to produce goods/services:
| Factor | Meaning | Reward earned |
|---|---|---|
| **Land** | Natural resources (physical land, raw materials, water) | Rent |
| **Labour** | Human effort, physical and mental | Wages |
| **Capital** | Man-made aids to production (machines, tools, factories) | Interest |
| **Enterprise** | The entrepreneur who takes risk and combines the other three | Profit |

**Adding value**
- **Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials/inputs.**
- It is the increase in worth a business creates as inputs are turned into outputs.
- Ways to add value: branding, better quality, convenience, speed, design, customer service, unique features.
- Higher added value → potential for higher profit and stronger competitive position. It does **not** automatically mean higher profit (other costs like labour still apply).

**Economic activity, choice and opportunity cost**
- Resources are **scarce** but wants are **infinite** → the fundamental **economic problem**.
- Scarcity forces **choice**.
- **Opportunity cost** = the benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
- Example: money spent on new machinery cannot be spent on marketing.

**The dynamic business environment**
- The business environment is constantly changing: consumer tastes, technology, competition, economy, laws, social attitudes.
- Businesses must be **flexible and adaptable** to survive; failing to respond to change is a common cause of failure.

**Why businesses succeed or fail**
- **Succeed:** good management, sufficient finance/cash flow, effective marketing, competitive products, responding to change, skilled workforce.
- **Fail:** poor cash-flow/financial management, lack of demand, poor management decisions, too much competition, failure to adapt, over-expansion.

**Local, national, international, multinational businesses**
| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Local** | Operates in a small area (one town/region), serving a local market |
| **National** | Operates throughout one country |
| **International** | Sells/trades in more than one country (exports) |
| **Multinational (MNC)** | Has production/operations bases in more than one country |

### 1.1.2 The role of entrepreneurs and intrapreneurs

**Entrepreneur** = a person who takes the risk of setting up and running a business, combining the factors of production.

**Intrapreneur** = an employee within an existing business who uses entrepreneurial skills to develop new products/processes for that business (innovation from within).

**Qualities needed (both):** innovation/creativity, risk-taking, self-confidence, determination/resilience, hard work, effective communication, leadership, self-motivation, ability to spot opportunities, multi-skilled.

**Role of entrepreneurship** — creating and starting up a business: spotting a market opportunity, gathering finance and resources, taking risk, organising production, driving the start-up.

**Role of intrapreneurship** — ongoing success of an existing business: generates innovation, new products and processes, keeps the business competitive and dynamic without the individual bearing the personal financial risk.

**Barriers to entrepreneurship:**
- Lack of finance / difficulty raising capital.
- Lack of business skills, knowledge or experience.
- High competition from established firms.
- Fear of failure / risk of losing personal assets.
- Legal/bureaucratic requirements and red tape.
- Economic conditions (recession), lack of infrastructure.

**Business risk vs uncertainty:**
- **Risk** = outcomes that can be identified and the probability estimated (can be planned for/insured).
- **Uncertainty** = unpredictable events that cannot be foreseen or measured (e.g. sudden pandemic, war, natural disaster).

**Role of business enterprise in a country's development:**
- Creates **jobs** and reduces unemployment.
- Increases **output/GDP** and economic growth.
- Encourages **innovation** and use of new technology.
- Raises **living standards** and government **tax revenue**.
- Increases exports and can attract investment.

### 1.1.3 Business plans

**Business plan** = a document describing a business, its objectives, strategies, the market it is in, and its financial forecasts.

**Purpose:** to clarify the business idea, set objectives, and — crucially — to **help raise finance** (banks/investors want to see one).

**Key elements:**
- Executive summary / description of the business idea.
- Products/services offered.
- Market/target customers and market research.
- Marketing plan (the 4Ps).
- Operations plan (production, location, premises).
- Management and personnel.
- Financial forecasts: cash flow forecast, projected profit, start-up costs, sources of finance.

**Benefits:** forces owner to research and think through the idea; helps obtain finance; sets targets to measure progress; identifies potential problems early; aids decision-making.

**Limitations:** based on **forecasts/assumptions** that may be wrong; environment changes quickly making it outdated; time-consuming to produce; a good plan does not guarantee success; may create false confidence.

## 1.2 Business structure

### 1.2.1 Economic sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Extracting natural resources | Farming, mining, fishing, oil extraction |
| **Secondary** | Manufacturing/processing raw materials into goods | Car making, construction, food processing |
| **Tertiary** | Providing services | Retail, banking, transport, tourism |
| **Quaternary** | Knowledge/information-based services | ICT, R&D, consultancy, data analysis |

- **Public sector:** owned/controlled by the government (state schools, public hospitals, defence).
- **Private sector:** owned by private individuals/businesses (aims usually profit).

**Changing relative importance of sectors:**
- **De-industrialisation:** in developed economies the secondary sector shrinks while tertiary/quaternary grow.
- Developing economies often shift from primary → secondary as they industrialise.
- **Reasons:** rising incomes (more demand for services), technology/automation, cheaper manufacturing abroad, growth of knowledge economy.
- **Consequences:** structural unemployment in declining sectors, need for retraining, changing skill demands, regional decline/growth.

### 1.2.2 Business ownership

**Unlimited liability** = owner is personally responsible for ALL business debts (personal assets at risk). Applies to sole traders and (ordinary) partnerships.

**Limited liability** = owners' losses are limited to the amount they invested; personal assets are protected. Applies to shareholders of Ltd and Plc. (Requires the business to be an **incorporated** = separate legal identity.)

| Type | Key features | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | One owner, unincorporated | Easy/cheap to set up, full control, keeps all profit, privacy | Unlimited liability, hard to raise finance, long hours, no continuity |
| **Partnership** | 2+ owners, deed of partnership | More capital, shared workload/skills, shared decisions | Unlimited liability (usually), profits shared, disagreements, no continuity |
| **Private limited company (Ltd)** | Shares sold privately, incorporated | Limited liability, more capital than sole trader, control kept within group, continuity | Shares can't be sold to public, must publish accounts, more admin/legal cost |
| **Public limited company (Plc)** | Shares sold on stock exchange | Limited liability, huge capital via share issue, status | Expensive to set up, must publish full accounts, risk of takeover, divorce of ownership & control, pressure for short-term profit |
| **Franchise** | Franchisee buys right to trade under franchisor's brand | Lower risk (proven idea), brand recognition, support/training | Fees & royalties, less independence, rules imposed by franchisor |
| **Co-operative** | Owned and run by members, one member one vote | Shared profits, democratic, motivated members | Limited capital, slow decisions, may lack management expertise |
| **Joint venture** | Two businesses form a separate business together | Shared costs/risk/expertise, access to new markets | Profits shared, culture clashes, disputes over management |
| **Social enterprise** | Trades to achieve social/environmental aims, profits reinvested | Meets social goals, may get support/grants, positive image | Harder to raise finance, may conflict profit vs social aims |

**Appropriateness:** small/new low-risk business → sole trader; family/professional business needing more capital → partnership or Ltd; large business needing major capital → Plc; proven-model low-experience owner → franchise.

**Changing ownership type (e.g. Ltd → Plc):**
- **Advantages:** raises far more capital, limited liability retained, greater status, growth possible.
- **Disadvantages:** loss of control, risk of takeover, must disclose accounts, divorce of ownership and control, short-termism pressure.

## 1.3 Size of business

### 1.3.1 Measurements of business size
Methods: **number of employees, revenue (sales turnover), capital employed, market share, market capitalisation** (Plc: share price × number of shares).

**Appropriateness:** no single measure is perfect.
- Number of employees misleads for capital-intensive firms (few staff, huge output).
- Revenue varies with price levels.
- Capital employed misleads for labour-intensive service firms.
- Best to use **several measures together** and compare like-with-like industries.

### 1.3.2 Significance of small businesses
**Small business advantages:** flexible/quick to respond, personal customer service, lower overheads, close staff relationships, can fill niche markets.
**Disadvantages:** hard to raise finance, no economies of scale, vulnerable to competition, reliance on few people, limited market.

**Family businesses — strengths:** commitment/loyalty, long-term view, shared values, trust, quick decisions.
**Weaknesses:** family conflict, succession problems, "jobs for the family" (unqualified relatives), resistance to outside ideas, difficulty separating family and business.

**Importance of small businesses to the economy:** create jobs, encourage competition and innovation, supply larger firms, provide local services, source of future large firms, offer variety/choice.

**Role in industrial structure:** in some industries (crafts, personal services, specialist consultancy) small firms dominate because personal service/customisation matters more than economies of scale.

### 1.3.3 Business growth

**Internal (organic) growth:** business grows using its own resources — more outlets, new products, more markets. Slower but lower risk, easier to manage, keeps control.

**External growth (merger/takeover):**
- **Merger:** two firms agree to join together (friendly).
- **Takeover (acquisition):** one firm buys a controlling interest in another. **Hostile takeover** = target company's board does not agree.

| Integration type | Meaning | Main benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | Same industry, same stage | More market share, economies of scale, less competition |
| **Vertical backward** | With supplier (earlier stage) | Control of supplies, quality, costs |
| **Vertical forward** | With customer/distributor (later stage) | Control of distribution/access to market |
| **Conglomerate (diversification)** | Different, unrelated industries | Spreads risk |

**Impact of merger/takeover on stakeholders:** possible job losses/redundancies (workers), price/choice changes (customers), higher returns or integration costs (shareholders), supplier changes.

**Why a merger/takeover may fail objectives:** culture clashes, poor integration, diseconomies of scale, overestimated synergies, high cost, staff resistance/low morale.

**Joint ventures & strategic alliances (external growth without full merger):**
- **Joint venture:** partners create a new separate entity.
- **Strategic alliance:** cooperation agreement without forming a new company.
- Benefits: share risk/cost/expertise, enter new markets, keep independence.

## 1.4 Business objectives

### 1.4.1 Objectives in private and public sector
**Private sector objectives:** survival (esp. start-ups/recession), profit maximisation, growth, increasing market share, protecting reputation/image.
**Public sector objectives:** provide a service, meet needs of community, stay within budget, high-quality service — not usually profit.
**Social enterprise objectives:** the **triple bottom line** (economic, social, environmental); profit is a means, not the end.

**Importance of objectives:** give direction/focus, aid decision-making, motivate staff, allow measurement of performance/success, help coordinate the business.

**CSR & Triple Bottom Line:**
- **CSR** = the idea that businesses have a responsibility to society and the environment beyond making profit.
- **Triple bottom line** = measuring success on three fronts:
  - **Profit** (economic/financial)
  - **People** (social)
  - **Planet** (environmental)

**Hierarchy — mission → aims → objectives → strategy → tactics:**
- **Mission statement:** the overall purpose/reason the business exists (qualitative).
- **Aims:** broad long-term goals.
- **Objectives:** specific, measurable goals derived from aims.
- **Strategy:** long-term plan to achieve objectives.
- **Tactics:** short-term actions to carry out the strategy.

### 1.4.2 Objectives and business decisions
- **Decision-making stages:** set objectives → gather data → analyse options → make decision → implement → review against objectives.
- **Objectives change over time:** start-up = survival; established = growth/profit; recession = survival again; mature = market share/diversification.
- **Translated into targets and budgets** for each department/team.
- **Communication of objectives** is essential so staff understand goals; can improve motivation (clarity, purpose) or reduce it if unrealistic/imposed.
- **SMART objectives:** **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-limited — makes objectives useful and reviewable.
- **Ethics & objectives:** ethical decisions (fair pay, no child labour, honest marketing) may raise costs/reduce short-term profit but improve reputation, loyalty and long-term success. There can be a **profit vs ethics trade-off**.

## 1.5 Stakeholders

### 1.5.1 Business stakeholders
**Stakeholder** = any individual/group with an interest in (affected by / can affect) the activities of a business.

| Internal stakeholders | External stakeholders |
|---|---|
| Owners/shareholders, managers, employees | Customers, suppliers, government, local community, banks/lenders, pressure groups, competitors |

**Roles, rights, responsibilities (examples):**
- Employees: provide labour; right to fair pay/safe conditions; responsibility to work well.
- Shareholders: provide capital; right to dividends/vote; responsibility to appoint directors.
- Customers: buy products; right to safe products/fair treatment.

### 1.5.2 Importance and influence of stakeholders
- Business decisions **impact stakeholders** and each reacts differently (e.g. cost-cutting → workers fear job loss, shareholders welcome higher profit).
- **Stakeholder aims influence decisions** — powerful stakeholders (major shareholders, key customers, government) have more influence.
- **Accountability:** businesses must answer to stakeholders (publish accounts, follow laws, report on CSR) — builds trust and reputation.
- **Stakeholder conflict:** aims often clash, e.g.:
  - Shareholders want higher profit ↔ employees want higher wages.
  - Owners want low costs ↔ community/environment wants clean, responsible operations.
  - Customers want low prices ↔ suppliers want higher prices.
- **Changing objectives affect stakeholders:** e.g. a shift to rapid growth may mean more jobs (good for workers) but lower short-term dividends (bad for shareholders).

# TOPIC 2 — HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT (HRM)

## 2.1 Human resource management

### 2.1.1 Purpose and roles of HRM
HRM = managing the workforce to meet organisational objectives. Roles: **workforce planning, recruitment & selection, training & development, appraisal, managing dismissal/redundancy, welfare and relations.**

### 2.1.2 Workforce planning
**Workforce plan** = identifying the number and skills of workers the business will need in the future, and how to meet that need.
**Reasons/role:** ensures right people in right jobs at right time; plans for expansion, new technology, retirements.

**Labour turnover** = % of workforce leaving in a year.
> **Labour turnover = (number leaving in a year ÷ average number employed) × 100**

- **High turnover implications:** higher recruitment/training costs, lost productivity, low morale — but may bring fresh ideas and lets business remove poor performers.
- **Low turnover implications:** experienced/stable workforce, lower costs — but may cause lack of new ideas and block promotion.

### 2.1.3 Recruitment and selection
**Recruitment process:**
- **Job description:** duties, tasks and responsibilities of the role.
- **Person specification:** the qualities, skills, qualifications a suitable candidate should have.

**Recruitment methods:** job advertisements, employment agencies, online recruitment.

| Internal recruitment | External recruitment |
|---|---|
| Filling a vacancy from existing staff | Recruiting from outside the business |
| + Cheaper, faster, known candidate, motivates staff | + Fresh ideas, wider pool, new skills |
| − Limited pool, no new ideas, creates another vacancy | − Slower, costlier, unknown candidate, longer induction |

**Selection methods:** curriculum vitae (CV)/résumé, application forms, interviews, references, testing (aptitude/psychometric), assessment centres.

**Employment contract** = legal document stating terms and conditions (pay, hours, duties, notice period).

### 2.1.4 Redundancy and dismissal
- **Redundancy:** the job no longer exists (not the worker's fault) — due to closure, restructuring, automation.
  - **Voluntary redundancy:** worker chooses to leave (often for a payment).
  - **Involuntary redundancy:** business selects who must leave.
- **Dismissal:** worker's contract ended because of their conduct/performance.
  - **Fair dismissal:** justified reason + correct procedure (e.g. gross misconduct, incompetence).
  - **Unfair dismissal:** no valid reason or correct procedure not followed (illegal).

### 2.1.5 Morale and welfare
- HRM affects **morale** (how positive/motivated staff feel) and **welfare** (their wellbeing).
- **Work-life balance:** helping staff balance work and personal life (flexible hours, remote work) → higher morale, lower turnover.
- **Diversity & equality:** a workforce with varied backgrounds treated fairly → wider talent, more ideas/creativity, better reputation, avoids discrimination claims, better serves diverse customers.

### 2.1.6 Training and development
| Type | Meaning | Pros/Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Induction** | Introduces new staff to the business/role | Settles staff quickly; costs time |
| **On-the-job** | Training while working (watching/doing) | Cheap, relevant; trainer less productive, bad habits passed on |
| **Off-the-job** | Training away from the workplace (courses) | Expert tuition, wide skills; expensive, may not be job-specific |

**Impact of training:** higher productivity/quality, better motivation, fewer accidents, more flexibility — but costs money/time and trained staff may leave.
- **Employee development** encourages **intrapreneurship** (staff innovate internally) and **multi-skilling & flexibility** (staff can do several roles, easier to cover absence and adapt).

### 2.1.7 Management and workforce relations
- **Cooperation** between management and workers benefits both: fewer disputes, higher productivity, better morale, shared problem-solving.
- **Trade union** = organisation representing workers' interests.
  - **Collective bargaining:** union negotiates pay/conditions on behalf of all members.
  - **Impact on employers:** slower decisions, possible strikes, but one point of negotiation and clearer communication.
  - **Impact on employees:** stronger negotiating power, protection, better pay/conditions.

## 2.2 Motivation

### 2.2.1 Why motivate?
Motivated workers are more productive, produce higher quality, have lower absenteeism and turnover, and help achieve business objectives.

### 2.2.2 & 2.2.3 Human needs & Motivation theories

**Content theories** (what motivates people):

- **F.W. Taylor (Scientific Management):** workers motivated mainly by **money**. Break jobs into simple tasks, pay **piece rate** (paid per unit). → higher output but treats workers like machines, ignores social needs.

- **Elton Mayo (Human Relations):** the **Hawthorne effect** — workers are motivated by **social needs, attention and teamwork**, not just pay. Group working and involvement raise productivity.

- **Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs** (satisfied bottom-up):
  1. Physiological (pay for food/shelter)
  2. Safety (job security, safe conditions)
  3. Social (belonging, teamwork)
  4. Esteem (recognition, status)
  5. Self-actualisation (achieving full potential)
  → Managers should identify which level a worker is on and offer the next level up.

- **Herzberg — Two-Factor Theory:**
  - **Hygiene factors** (pay, conditions, supervision, security): if poor cause **dissatisfaction**, but improving them does **not motivate**.
  - **Motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement): these truly motivate → **job enrichment**.

- **McClelland — Three Needs:** people driven by **Achievement, Affiliation (relationships), Power** in differing amounts; managers should match roles to the dominant need.

**Process theory** (how motivation works):
- **Vroom — Expectancy Theory:** Motivation = **Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence**. People are motivated if they believe effort → good performance (expectancy), performance → reward (instrumentality), and they value that reward (valence). If any element is zero, motivation is zero.

### 2.2.4 Motivation methods in practice

**Financial motivators (payment methods):**
| Method | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Time-based (wage)** | Paid per hour/time worked |
| **Salary** | Fixed annual amount, paid monthly |
| **Piece rate** | Paid per unit produced |
| **Commission** | % of sales made |
| **Bonus** | Extra payment for hitting targets |
| **Profit sharing** | Share of company profit to staff |
| **Performance-related pay (PRP)** | Pay linked to appraised performance |
| **Fringe benefits (perks)** | Non-cash extras: car, health insurance, discounts |

**Non-financial motivators:** training, opportunities for promotion, development, status, **job re-design**, team working, **empowerment** (giving control), participation, **job enrichment** (adding challenging/meaningful tasks), job enlargement, job rotation.

**Employee participation in management/control:** works councils, worker directors, quality circles, team meetings, share-ownership schemes.

## 2.3 Management

### 2.3.1 Managers
**Traditional functions:** **Planning, Organising, Directing (commanding/coordinating), Controlling.**

- **Henri Fayol — five functions:** planning, organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling.
- **Henry Mintzberg — managerial roles** in three groups:
  - **Interpersonal:** figurehead, leader, liaison.
  - **Informational:** monitor, disseminator, spokesperson.
  - **Decisional:** entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator.

**Contribution of managers to performance:** set direction, coordinate resources, motivate staff, make decisions, control quality/costs.

**Management styles:**
| Style | Description | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| **Autocratic** | Manager makes all decisions, one-way orders | Crisis, unskilled staff, urgency |
| **Democratic** | Decisions shared, staff consulted | Skilled/creative staff, complex problems |
| **Laissez-faire** | Staff left to make own decisions | Highly skilled, motivated experts |
| **Paternalistic** | Manager decides but acts in staff's best interest, consults | Staff need guidance but valued |

**McGregor — Theory X and Theory Y:**
- **Theory X:** managers assume workers are lazy, dislike work, avoid responsibility → need close control (autocratic).
- **Theory Y:** managers assume workers enjoy work, are creative and seek responsibility → empower and involve them (democratic).

# TOPIC 3 — MARKETING

## 3.1 The nature of marketing

### 3.1.1 Role of marketing
**Marketing** = anticipating, identifying and satisfying customer needs profitably.
- **Marketing objectives:** goals of the marketing dept (increase sales, market share, brand awareness, launch products, enter new markets).
- **Link to corporate objectives:** marketing objectives must support the overall corporate objectives (e.g. corporate goal "growth" → marketing goal "increase market share by 10%").

### 3.1.2 Demand and supply
- **Demand** = quantity customers are willing/able to buy at a given price.
  - Factors affecting demand: price, incomes, price of substitutes/complements, tastes/fashion, advertising, season.
- **Supply** = quantity producers are willing to provide at a given price.
  - Factors affecting supply: costs of production, technology, taxes/subsidies, weather (for primary goods), number of producers.
- **Interaction:** where demand = supply → **equilibrium price**. Excess demand pushes price up; excess supply pushes price down.

### 3.1.3 Markets
- **Consumer markets:** goods/services sold to final consumers.
- **Industrial markets:** goods/services sold to other businesses.
- **Local / national / international markets** — geographic scope.
- **Product orientation:** focus on making the product then finding buyers (product-led). Works for technical/innovative goods.
- **Customer (market) orientation:** research customer needs first, then produce to match. Lower risk, more responsive.
- **Market share = (business's sales ÷ total market sales) × 100**
- **Market growth = (change in market size ÷ original market size) × 100**
- **Implications:** rising market share = competitive strength/possible economies of scale; rising market growth = attractive market but may draw new entrants.

### 3.1.4 Consumer and industrial marketing
- **Product classification:** consumer goods (convenience, shopping, speciality), consumer services, and industrial/producer goods.
- **B2C (business to consumer):** mass advertising, emotional appeal, branding, retail channels.
- **B2B (business to business):** fewer, larger buyers; rational/technical appeal; personal selling; long-term relationships; negotiated pricing.

### 3.1.5 Mass vs niche marketing
| | Mass marketing | Niche marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Whole/large market, one product | Small specific segment |
| Pros | Economies of scale, high sales volume, brand awareness | Less competition, higher prices/margins, loyal customers, focused |
| Cons | High competition, high setup cost, inflexible | Small sales volume, vulnerable if segment shrinks, limited growth |

### 3.1.6 Market segmentation
Dividing a market into groups with similar characteristics.
- **Geographic:** by region/country/climate.
- **Demographic:** by age, gender, income, occupation, family, religion.
- **Psychographic:** by lifestyle, values, personality, attitudes.

**Advantages:** target marketing, meet needs better, higher sales, identify gaps, less waste.
**Disadvantages:** research cost, may misjudge segment, extra production/marketing cost, segment may be too small.

### 3.1.7 Customer relationship marketing (CRM)
- **Aim:** build long-term loyal relationships with customers to increase **customer retention** and lifetime value.
- **Benefits:** repeat sales, loyalty, word-of-mouth, cheaper than winning new customers, useful data.
- **Costs:** technology/database systems, staff training, loyalty scheme costs.

## 3.2 Market research

### 3.2.1 Purpose
Identify market features (size, growth, competitors) and customer/consumer characteristics, profiles, wants and needs → reduces risk in decisions.

### 3.2.2 Primary vs secondary research
- **Primary (field) research:** new, first-hand data collected for a specific purpose. Methods: surveys/questionnaires, interviews, observation, focus groups, test marketing.
  - **Useful:** up-to-date, specific, exclusive to the business. **Limitations:** expensive, time-consuming, risk of bias/small sample.
- **Secondary (desk) research:** existing data collected by others. Sources: government stats, market reports, internet, internal records, media.
  - **Useful:** cheaper, quick, broad. **Limitations:** may be out of date, not specific, available to competitors.

### 3.2.3 Sampling
- **Sample** = a group selected to represent the whole population.
- **Need:** cannot ask everyone — saves time/cost.
- **Limitations:** if too small or unrepresentative → **sampling bias** and unreliable results.
- Types to know: random, quota, stratified.

### 3.2.4 Market research data
- **Reliability** depends on sample size, method, honesty of respondents, how up-to-date the data is.
- **Quantitative data:** numerical (how many, how much) — easy to analyse statistically.
- **Qualitative data:** opinions/reasons (why) — deeper insight, harder to measure.
- **Interpretation:** be able to read tables, bar charts, pie charts, line graphs and draw conclusions.

## 3.3 The marketing mix (4Ps)

### 3.3.1 The 4Ps
**Product, Price, Promotion, Place** — must work together consistently and suit the target market.

### 3.3.2 Product
- **Goods** = tangible; **Services** = intangible.
- **Tangible attributes:** physical features, quality, design. **Intangible attributes:** brand image, reputation, after-sales service.
- **Product development** keeps the range fresh and competitive.
- **Product differentiation:** making a product distinct from rivals.
- **Unique Selling Point (USP):** the feature that makes a product stand out and gives a reason to buy.

### 3.3.3 Product portfolio analysis
**Product life cycle stages:** Development → Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline.
- **Extension strategies** (to prolong maturity): new packaging, new uses, new markets, price changes, promotion, product updates.

**Boston Matrix** (analyses a product portfolio by market share vs market growth):
| | High market share | Low market share |
|---|---|---|
| **High market growth** | **Star** (invest to grow) | **Question mark/Problem child** (decide: invest or drop) |
| **Low market growth** | **Cash cow** (milk for cash) | **Dog** (consider dropping) |

- **Use:** shows balance of portfolio; cash cows fund stars and question marks; helps plan marketing spend and product decisions.

### 3.3.4 Pricing methods
| Method | Meaning | Best used |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-based (cost-plus)** | Add a mark-up to unit cost | Simple, covers costs |
| **Competitive** | Price in line with rivals | Competitive markets |
| **Penetration** | Low price to enter market/gain share | New product launch |
| **Skimming** | High initial price then lower it | New, innovative/tech products |
| **Price discrimination** | Different prices to different groups/times | Where segments differ (e.g. peak/off-peak) |
| **Dynamic** | Price changes with demand in real time | Airlines, hotels, e-commerce |
| **Psychological** | e.g. $9.99 to seem cheaper | Retail |

### 3.3.5 Promotion methods
Aim: inform, persuade, remind → increase sales/awareness.
- **Advertising:** paid non-personal media (TV, print, online) — builds awareness; expensive.
- **Sales promotion:** short-term incentives (discounts, BOGOF, coupons, loyalty points) — boosts short-term sales.
- **Direct promotion:** direct mail, email, telemarketing to targeted customers.
- **Digital promotion (developments):** social media, influencer marketing, SEO, pay-per-click, viral/content marketing — cheap, wide reach, targeted, measurable.
- **Packaging in promotion:** protects, informs, attracts attention, reinforces brand.
- **Branding in promotion:** creates recognition, loyalty, allows premium pricing, differentiates.
- **Above-the-line** = paid mass media (advertising). **Below-the-line** = other methods the business controls (sales promotion, direct, PR).

### 3.3.6 Place (channels of distribution)
Getting the product to the customer in the right place at the right time.
- **Channels:** Producer → Consumer (direct); Producer → Retailer → Consumer; Producer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer.
- **Physical distribution:** shops, stores, physical delivery.
- **Digital distribution:** online sales, downloads, e-commerce, apps — wider reach, lower cost, 24/7.
- Choice depends on product type, market, cost, control needed.

# TOPIC 4 — OPERATIONS MANAGEMENT

## 4.1 The nature of operations

### 4.1.1 The transformational process
- Operations use the **factors of production** (land, labour, capital, enterprise) to turn **inputs → outputs**.
- **Inputs** (raw materials, labour, machines) → **Process** (transformation) → **Outputs** (finished goods/services).
- Operations **add value** by turning inputs into higher-worth outputs.

### 4.1.2 Efficiency, effectiveness, productivity, sustainability
- **Efficiency:** producing with minimum waste/cost (output per unit of input).
- **Effectiveness:** meeting objectives/customer needs (doing the right things).
- **Productivity:** output per unit of input in a period.
- **Sustainability:** operating without harming the ability of future generations (environment).

> **Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of employees** (per period)

- **Improving sustainability** (recycling, renewable energy, less waste): + better image, lower long-term costs, meets regulation; − high initial cost, may raise prices.

### 4.1.3 Capital vs labour intensive
| | Capital intensive | Labour intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Relies mainly on machinery | Relies mainly on workers |
| Pros | Consistent quality, high output, lower long-run cost, works 24/7 | Flexible, lower setup cost, human judgement/creativity, personal service |
| Cons | High setup cost, inflexible, breakdowns, less personal | Wage costs, variable quality, absence/turnover, training needed |

### 4.1.4 Operations methods
| Method | Meaning | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Job** | One-off, custom product | Meets exact needs, high quality/price | Slow, expensive, labour-intensive |
| **Batch** | Groups of identical products in stages | Flexible, some economies of scale | Downtime between batches, inventory storage |
| **Flow** | Continuous mass production | Low unit cost, fast, economies of scale | Very high setup cost, inflexible, boring work |
| **Mass customisation** | Flow methods but tailored to customers | Choice + economies of scale | Complex, needs flexible tech/skills |

- **Problems of changing method:** cost of new equipment, retraining, disruption to production, staff resistance/redundancy.

## 4.2 Inventory management

### 4.2.1 Managing inventory
- **Types of inventory:** raw materials, work in progress, finished goods.
- **Benefits of holding inventory:** meet demand, avoid stock-outs, cope with delays, allow production to continue.
- **Costs of holding inventory:** storage/warehouse cost, tied-up capital (opportunity cost), risk of waste/obsolescence/theft.

**Inventory control chart terms:**
- **Buffer inventory:** minimum stock held as a safety margin.
- **Re-order level:** stock level at which a new order is placed.
- **Lead time:** time between placing and receiving an order.
- **Maximum stock level:** the most that can/should be held.
- Read the "saw-tooth" chart: stock falls as used, jumps up when delivery arrives.

- **Supply Chain Management (SCM):** managing the flow of goods from supplier to customer efficiently → lower costs, reliable supply, better quality.

### 4.2.2 Just in Time (JIT) vs Just in Case (JIC)
- **JIT:** inputs arrive exactly when needed; little/no inventory held.
  - + Reduces storage costs, less waste, frees up capital, improves cash flow.
  - − No buffer → vulnerable to supply delays, needs reliable suppliers, no economies of bulk buying.
- **JIC:** holds buffer stock in case of demand surges/supply problems.
  - + Never runs out, can meet sudden demand; − high storage cost, tied-up capital, waste risk.

## 4.3 Capacity utilisation and outsourcing

### 4.3.1 Capacity utilisation
> **Capacity utilisation = (actual output ÷ maximum possible output) × 100**

- **Under-utilisation (low %):** high fixed cost per unit, wasted resources, staff may feel insecure — but room to grow, can meet sudden orders.
- **Over-utilisation (near/at 100%):** low unit costs, but no room for maintenance/repairs, staff stress, quality may fall, can't meet extra orders.
- **Improving utilisation:** increase sales/demand (marketing), reduce capacity (rationalisation), subcontract work in, cut prices, new products.

### 4.3.2 Outsourcing
Using an outside firm to carry out tasks previously done in-house.
- + Lower costs, access to specialist skills, flexibility, focus on core activities.
- − Less control over quality, reliability risk, communication issues, loss of confidentiality, possible job losses (morale).

# TOPIC 5 — FINANCE AND ACCOUNTING

## 5.1 Business finance

### 5.1.1 Need for business finance
- **Start-up:** premises, equipment, initial stock, legal costs.
- **Growth/expansion:** new premises, machinery, R&D, marketing.
- **Survival/day-to-day:** wages, rent, raw materials, bills.
- **Short-term finance:** to fund day-to-day/working capital (overdraft, trade credit).
- **Long-term finance:** to fund non-current assets/expansion (loans, share capital, mortgages).

**Cash vs profit:**
- **Cash** = money available now to pay bills (liquidity).
- **Profit** = revenue − total costs over a period.
- A business can be **profitable but run out of cash** (e.g. customers pay late, money tied in stock) → this causes failure.

**Business failure from lack of finance:**
- **Bankruptcy:** an individual (e.g. sole trader) is legally declared unable to pay debts.
- **Liquidation:** a company is closed, assets sold to repay creditors.
- **Administration:** an insolvent company is run by an administrator to try to rescue it/repay creditors.

### 5.1.2 Working capital
> **Working capital = current assets − current liabilities**
- Finance available for **day-to-day** running. Too little → can't pay bills (illiquid); too much → idle resources.
- **Managing trade receivables** (money owed by customers): collect promptly, credit checks, discounts for early payment.
- **Managing trade payables** (money owed to suppliers): negotiate longer credit to keep cash longer (without harming relations).
- **Capital expenditure:** spending on non-current (fixed) assets (machinery, buildings) — long-term.
- **Revenue expenditure:** spending on day-to-day running (wages, materials, rent) — short-term.

## 5.2 Sources of finance

### 5.2.1 Ownership & finance
- Sole traders/partnerships: limited to owner savings, loans, retained profit — harder to raise large sums.
- Ltd: can sell shares privately. Plc: can sell shares to public (largest access to finance).

### 5.2.2 Internal vs external sources
**Internal (from within the business):**
- Owner's investment (personal savings).
- Retained earnings (reinvested profit).
- Sale of unwanted assets.
- Sale and leaseback (sell an asset then lease it back).
- Working capital (managing it better).

**External (from outside):**
- **Share capital** (sell shares) · **Debentures** (long-term loans/bonds) · **New partners** · **Venture capital** (investment for high-risk firms) · **Bank overdraft** · **Leasing** · **Hire purchase** · **Bank loans** · **Mortgages** · **Debt factoring** (sell receivables to a factor for immediate cash) · **Trade credit** · **Micro-finance** (small loans, developing economies) · **Crowdfunding** · **Government grants**.

### 5.2.3 Factors affecting choice of source
- **Cost** (interest, fees) · **Flexibility** · **Need to retain control** (issuing shares dilutes control) · **Use/purpose** (short-term need → short-term source; long-term asset → long-term source) · **Level of existing debt (gearing)** · legal form of business · amount needed.

### 5.2.4 Selecting the source
Match the source to the situation: e.g. new machinery (long-term) → bank loan/leasing; short cash-flow gap → overdraft; large expansion for a Plc → share issue; a start-up sole trader → savings/micro-finance/crowdfunding.

## 5.3 Forecasting and managing cash flow

### 5.3.1 Cash flow forecasts
**Cash flow forecast** = a prediction of the cash coming in (inflows) and going out (outflows) over a future period.
**Purpose:** spot cash shortages early, arrange finance in advance, plan spending, support a loan application.

**Key calculations:**
> **Net cash flow = total cash inflows − total cash outflows**
> **Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**
> **Opening balance = previous month's closing balance**

**Improving cash flow:**
- Speed up inflows: chase receivables, offer early-payment discounts, reduce credit period, sell assets.
- Slow outflows: negotiate longer credit with suppliers, lease instead of buy, delay non-essential spending.
- Arrange overdraft/short-term loan; reduce stock held.

## 5.4 Costs

### 5.4.1 Cost information
Accurate costs are needed for pricing, monitoring performance, and decision-making.
- **Fixed costs:** do not change with output (rent, salaries, insurance).
- **Variable costs:** change directly with output (raw materials, piece-rate wages).
- **Direct costs:** clearly linked to a product (materials for that product).
- **Indirect costs (overheads):** not linked to one product (admin, rent).
> **Total cost = fixed costs + variable costs** &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Total variable cost = variable cost per unit × output**
> **Average cost (unit cost) = total cost ÷ output**

### 5.4.2 Full vs contribution costing
- **Full (absorption) costing:** allocates ALL costs (direct + a share of overheads) to each product.
  - Use: ensures all costs covered when pricing. Limitation: overheads shared using arbitrary methods → can distort.
- **Contribution costing (marginal costing):** only allocates direct/variable costs to products; treats fixed costs as a total.
> **Contribution per unit = selling price per unit − variable cost per unit**
> **Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold**
> **Profit = total contribution − fixed costs**
- **Contribution vs profit:** contribution goes first towards covering fixed costs; only what is left after fixed costs are covered is profit.
- **Limitations of contribution costing:** ignores how fixed costs are shared, less useful for full-price setting.
- **When used:** special order decisions, make-or-buy, whether to accept a low-price order, comparing products.

### 5.4.3 Uses of cost information
- **Decision-making:** average, marginal, total costs.
- **Pricing:** costs set the minimum price (cost-plus).
- **Monitoring performance:** compare actual vs budgeted costs, calculate profit.
- **Special order decisions (contribution):** accept an order if the price is **above variable cost per unit** (so it makes a positive contribution) — provided spare capacity exists and normal sales aren't harmed.

### 5.4.4 Break-even analysis
**Break-even point** = the level of output where **total revenue = total costs** (no profit, no loss).

> **Break-even output (units) = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit**
> **Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit**
> **Total revenue = selling price × quantity sold**
> **Margin of safety = actual (current) output − break-even output**
> **Profit = (contribution per unit × units sold) − fixed costs**

**Break-even chart:** x-axis = output, y-axis = costs/revenue. Lines: fixed cost (horizontal), total cost, total revenue. Break-even point = where total revenue crosses total cost. Margin of safety = distance from break-even to actual output.

**Uses:** shows break-even level, how much output needed for target profit, effect of price/cost changes, margin of safety.
**Limitations:** assumes all output is sold, assumes costs/price are constant (unrealistic), fixed costs may change (step costs), ignores changing conditions, only as good as the data used.

## 5.5 Budgets

### 5.5.1 Meaning and purpose
**Budget** = a financial plan/target for a future period.
**Uses:** measure performance, allocate resources, control and monitor the business, set targets, coordinate departments.

- **Benefits:** control spending, motivate (if targets agreed), aids planning/coordination, identifies problems early, measures performance.
- **Drawbacks:** time-consuming, may be inaccurate/based on guesses, can demotivate if unrealistic/imposed, may cause rivalry between departments, can be inflexible.

**Types of budget:**
- **Incremental budget:** based on last period's budget adjusted (up/down) by a % — simple but continues past errors.
- **Flexible budget:** adjusts with the actual level of output/activity — more realistic.
- **Zero-based budgeting (ZBB):** every budget starts from zero; all spending must be justified each period — avoids waste but time-consuming.

### 5.5.2 Variances
**Variance** = the difference between the budgeted figure and the actual figure.
- **Favourable variance:** actual result is better for the business (actual revenue higher, or actual costs lower than budget).
- **Adverse (unfavourable) variance:** actual result is worse (actual revenue lower, or actual costs higher than budget).

> **Variance = actual figure − budgeted figure** (then judge favourable/adverse by its effect on profit)
- **Interpretation:** use variances to investigate causes and take corrective action.
